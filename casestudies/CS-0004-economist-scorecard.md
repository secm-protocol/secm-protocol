# CS-0004 — The Economist Scorecard: What Institutional Forecasting Actually Achieves

Produced 2026-07-25. **Pre-registered hypothesis and method stated before data collection
concluded.** Subject: The Economist's "The World Ahead" annual (2026 = 40th edition) and,
more importantly, the calibrated-forecasting infrastructure attached to it.

## Pre-registered hypothesis (stated before scoring)

**H1.** The publication achieves high accuracy on **continuity themes** (an ongoing war
continues, a rivalry persists, a growing industry grows) and low accuracy on
**discontinuities** (regime changes, crises, ruptures) — which are precisely the events
that carry asymmetric financial payoff.

**H2.** No public accuracy scorecard exists for the publication's own forecasts.

## Finding 1 — H2 CONFIRMED, and this is the headline

Searches across the indexed web return **no published accuracy scorecard, no
retrospective "we were wrong" audit, and no documented hit-rate** for The World Ahead's
forecasts across its 40 editions.

Forty years of annual predictions. Zero public accountability ledger.

This is the identical structural failure documented in CS-0001 (viral prophet) and
formalized in PROPHET-AUDIT — appearing at the most prestigious tier of institutional
forecasting. The mechanism is not fraud; it is that **nobody, at any level of prestige,
volunteers a denominator.** Prediction sets are published; resolution sets are not.

## Finding 2 — the elasticity problem, measured on the 2026 cover

The cover's own stated agenda: *Geopolitical drift · Opportunities for China · After
Gaza · Outlook for the Middle East · Beyond Ukraine · Russia's next moves · The global
economy: Is a crisis brewing? · Sporting values · Political football · Weight-loss
drugs · The next generation.*

Classified by falsifiability:

| Theme | Falsifiable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Is a crisis brewing?" | **No** | Interrogative form cannot resolve false |
| "Geopolitical drift" | **No** | No threshold, no date |
| "Opportunities for China" | **No** | Opportunities always exist |
| "Russia's next moves" | **No** | Framing, not a claim |
| "After Gaza" | Partially | Depends on undefined end-state |
| "Weight-loss drugs: next generation" | **Yes** | Approvals are dated public events |

**Of eleven headline themes, one is cleanly scoreable.** A publication cannot be graded
for accuracy on statements that were never falsifiable — which is simultaneously why it
is rarely wrong and why its cover carries little tradable information. The visual
elements are weaker still: tanks, missiles, satellites, syringes and dollar signs have
occurred in every year of the past eighty.

The single non-obvious numeral, **"250" inside the brain**, resolves prosaically: 2026
is the 250th anniversary of US independence (1776). Legend, not cipher.

## Finding 3 — the real signal was never the cover

The Economist has run an annual prediction challenge with **Good Judgment Open for 11
consecutive years** — the public arm of the Good Judgment Project. The 2026 edition
carries ~25 questions with **explicit resolution dates and public crowd forecasts**,
with 350–1,400 forecasters per question. Examples (all closing 2026):

- NVIDIA market cap on 2026-08-28 (1,148 forecasters)
- Trump Nobel Peace Prize in 2026 (1,387 forecasters)
- IDF full withdrawal from Gaza before 2026-10-01 (766 forecasters)
- PT candidate first-round vote share, Brazil (652 forecasters)
- Sudan ceasefire lasting 28+ days (765 forecasters)
- SpaceX in-orbit Starship fuel transfer before 2026-10-01 (817 forecasters)
- Gold outperforming silver, Apr 2 → Aug 28 2026 (601 forecasters)

**This is the opposite of the cover in every structural respect:** falsifiable claims,
fixed resolution dates, numeric probabilities, public participation, scored outcomes.
The magazine's genuinely rigorous forecasting product is the part nobody screenshots.

## Finding 4 — the documented ceiling of elite forecasting

The Good Judgment Project's IARPA-tournament results (4 years, ~500 questions):

- Superforecasters were **~30% more accurate than professional intelligence analysts
  with access to classified material**.
- Their 400-day-ahead accuracy matched ordinary forecasters' 150-day-ahead accuracy.
- Accuracy predictors were **cognitive ability, political knowledge and
  open-mindedness** — not access to secrets.

Two implications that settle the entire question this case study was built to answer:

1. **The best measured human forecasters beat classified-intelligence professionals by
   ~30% — not by 300%.** Even elite calibrated forecasting is a modest edge over
   base rates, not clairvoyance. Any operator or publication implying near-perfect
   foresight is describing something no measured population has ever achieved.
2. **The edge came from method, not privileged information.** Which means it is
   reproducible by anyone willing to make falsifiable claims and keep score — and is
   structurally unavailable to anyone who won't.

## Conclusion

**H1 remains unscoreable** — not because the data is missing, but because the cover's
claims are mostly unfalsifiable by construction. That is itself the finding: the
famous artefact is prestige, the attached challenge is method.

**Operational reading:** The World Ahead maps **elite consensus**, which is valuable for
knowing what coordinated actors expect (EE-004 intent signals) and therefore what is
already priced. It is not a source of asymmetry. The tradable question is never "what
does the cover show" but "where does measurable, calibrated crowd probability diverge
from market pricing" — and that source is public, free, and ignored.

## Actionable follow-through (falsifiable, resolves within 2026)

The Good Judgment Open crowd forecasts on the questions above are public and dated.
Where a liquid market exists for the same event (NVIDIA market cap, Nobel odds,
prediction markets), the **divergence between calibrated crowd forecast and market
price** is a measurable object — exactly divergence rule D6 of EE-004. This is the
first live application of the intent/divergence framework to a public dataset.
